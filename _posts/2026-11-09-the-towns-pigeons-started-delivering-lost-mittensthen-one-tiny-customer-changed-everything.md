By [Dr. Priya Whiskerworth]({{ 'about.html#dr-priya-whiskerworth' | relative_url }})

At 8:03 on Monday morning, the people of Bellwether found a mitten on the town hall steps. It was followed by another mitten, then six more, arranged in a neat little semicircle around a pigeon wearing what appeared to be a very serious expression.

By lunchtime, the town had discovered that its pigeons were returning lost mittens. The birds left each one beside the spot where its owner had last been seen wearing the matching glove. No one knew how they managed it. Bellwether’s mayor called it “a public-service miracle with feathers.”

I consulted Professor Niblet, the local expert in speculative science and snack-related behavior. “Pigeons are excellent at noticing patterns,” she explained. “Also, they notice when people drop crumbs. Both skills may be relevant.” For background, pigeons have a long history of impressive navigation; see the [Wikipedia overview of the homing pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homing_pigeon). Professor Niblet added that the birds’ motivation might include “community spirit, curiosity, or a tiny hope of receiving a biscuit.”

The mitten operation quickly became an all-town effort. Residents set up a cheerful lost-and-found table, while the pigeons formed what looked like a delivery queue. One bird carried a striped mitten. Another proudly presented a woolly glove so small that it could only belong to a toddler—or a very ambitious hamster.

Then came the twist. On Wednesday, a little girl named Bea arrived at the table holding a single blue mitten. “I lost the other one,” she said. “But I don’t need it back. I want to give this one to the pigeon who looks cold.”

The pigeon in question, a round fellow with one white feather sticking up like a feathered fountain, accepted the mitten by sitting beside it. He did not put it on. Pigeons, it turns out, have limited enthusiasm for finger compartments.

Bea’s idea inspired a new project: a basket of tiny, bird-safe nesting materials, prepared with advice from the town’s wildlife volunteers. The mitten flock became the “Warm Perch Club,” and every Saturday families gathered to help local birds in sensible, wildlife-friendly ways. The original mitten was placed beside the basket as the club’s unofficial mascot.

By the end of the week, Bellwether had reunited 42 people with their missing mittens, discovered one lost scarf, and appointed a pigeon as honorary assistant to the municipal lost-and-found. He is paid in approved birdseed and receives excellent reviews.

As Professor Niblet put it: “Sometimes science begins with a question. Sometimes it begins with a pigeon and a suspiciously well-organized glove.”
